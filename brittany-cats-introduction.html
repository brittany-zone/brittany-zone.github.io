Brittany and Cats: How to Introduce a Bird Dog to a Cat or Kitten and Manage Chasing at Home

A calm, staged plan that keeps your cat safe and your bird dog in control.

Home›Blog›Brittany and cats

Bringing a Brittany and cats together in one home can feel like a gamble, because you are asking a bred-for-birds pointing dog to share the sofa with a small animal that runs. The good news is that you stay in control of every step: with separate spaces, a leash, short calm sessions and a clear rule for when to move forward, most households can reach a peaceful routine. This guide walks you through that process, from the first scent swap to the first unleashed evening, at a pace that keeps both animals safe.

Quick answer: can a Brittany live safely with a cat or kitten?

Often yes, but not by just putting them in the same room and hoping. A Brittany can learn to live calmly with a cat when the cat has dog-free escape routes, the dog meets the cat on a leash many times before any free contact, and every stage moves forward only when both animals stay relaxed.

TL;DR: Keep the cat in a dog-free room with food, water, litter and high perches for the first days. Swap scents and rooms before any face-to-face meeting. Hold the first meetings when your Brittany is calm after a walk, on a leash, with a gate or crate between them. Reward your dog for looking away from the cat. Move one step forward only after several calm sessions, step back after any lunge or chase, and never leave the two alone together until you are certain your dog won't chase.

Why does a Brittany chase cats?

Because chasing small, fast-moving animals is normal dog behavior, and a Brittany was bred to search for, find and point game birds. A cat that bolts across the floor looks a lot like the movement the breed was selected to notice.

Chase is a normal instinct, not bad manners

The overview of cat–dog relationships on Wikipedia sums it up plainly: dogs have a natural instinct to chase smaller animals that flee, and cats share that instinct. A running cat therefore invites a chase from almost any dog. With a Brittany, you may see the classic bird-dog sequence first: head up, body still, eyes locked on the target. That frozen stare is your early warning, and it is much easier to interrupt than a dog already in full sprint.

If you already practice redirecting your dog away from birds on walks, you have a head start. The same skill from our guide on teaching a walk-away cue when your Brittany points on leash transfers well indoors: notice the stare, say the cue, and reward your dog for turning back to you.

Play chase versus predatory chase

Not every chase means danger, and that should lower your anxiety a little. The San Francisco SPCA's dog-and-cat introduction handout explains that dogs who are not used to cats tend to treat them either like another dog (play, investigation, posturing) or like prey (chasing). It adds that most dogs that chase cats are not truly predatory and do no physical damage if they catch or corner the cat, although the stress for the cat is still real. The same handout is honest about the other end: dogs can and do sometimes injure and kill cats, and predation is a deeply ingrained trait that a dog cannot easily be trained out of.

That is why the plan below never relies on hoping your dog is "just playing". You manage both possibilities the same way: leash, distance, rewards, and slow progress.

Is your household a good fit before you start?

Look at both animals honestly first. The best sign is a dog who has already lived peacefully with a cat; the next best is a calm trial meeting on a leash, where your Brittany investigates cautiously and backs off when the cat signals.

Signs that point to a good prospect

The SF SPCA handout lists good signs during a trial meeting: cautious investigation, wagging, and respect for the cat's defensive signals, such as backing off. Bad signs are instant attempts to chase, out-of-control straining at the leash, whining, barking and agitation. Many dogs fall somewhere in the middle, which is normal and simply means more management and more patience.

Kitten or adult cat?

Both can work, and the experts weigh it slightly differently. The SF SPCA describes relaxed, laid-back cats and kittens as the best prospects, because they are less likely to flee and trigger a chase. The RSPCA Australia guide to introducing a new cat or kitten to the family dog suggests considering an adult cat if you have older pets, since older cats are usually calmer than kittens. In practice, temperament matters more than age: a confident, unhurried cat is easier for a Brittany than a nervous one who sprints at every sound.

How do you set up the house before the first meeting?

Give the cat a dog-free base with food, water, litter, a bed and high places to sit, and make sure your Brittany cannot reach the litter tray or cat food. Set this up before the new pet arrives, so nobody has to improvise in a stressful moment.

The cat's safe room

RSPCA Australia recommends a private, dog-free area with food, water, a litter tray, a bed, a hiding place and elevated platforms, and high resting places throughout the house so the cat can always get above the dog. The SF SPCA adds that the cat should be able to retreat and then come back into "dog territory" at her own pace, and that dogs should not have access to the litter box, both because it stresses the cat and because many dogs will eat litter and cat food. Feeding the cat in its safe room or on a high surface solves the food problem.

You will not be alone in separating meals. In a Polish survey of 87 people who owned both a cat and a dog, described by psychologist Stanley Coren in his Psychology Today column on dogs and cats sharing a home, 48 percent of owners placed the dog's and cat's bowls in different rooms.

Tools that keep you in control

  • Baby gates or pet gates in doorways, so the cat can see the dog without the dog getting through.
  • A crate your Brittany already likes. RSPCA Australia suggests crate training the dog before introductions.
  • A flat collar or harness and a standard leash for every early meeting.
  • A dog mat as a settle spot. If your dog does not know one yet, our guide to teaching a Brittany an off switch on a mat builds exactly the calm you need here.
  • A large cushion nearby, which RSPCA Australia suggests placing between the animals if needed.
  • Small, soft treats and a chew toy to keep your dog busy while the cat moves around.

Brittany and cat introduction, step by step

Work through six stages and stay at each one until both animals are calm. RSPCA Australia expects introductions to take at least a week and perhaps a few weeks, and the new cat's settling-in time alone may take several days or even a couple of weeks.

Stage 1: separate rooms

Let the cat settle in its safe room with everything it needs. Your Brittany carries on with normal life, walks and training. Use this time, as RSPCA Australia suggests, to brush up basic cues such as sit, down, a mat cue and "leave it".

Stage 2: scent and room swaps

Swap bedding between the animals. Then put the dog in a favorite room with its bed, water and toys, and let the cat explore the rest of the house; afterward, let the cat return to its room before the dog comes out. RSPCA Australia suggests repeating these swaps over several days.

Stage 3: seeing each other through a gate

Choose a moment when your dog is calmest; RSPCA Australia gives the example of after a long walk, and our guide to how much exercise a Brittany needs each day helps you plan that walk. Keep your Brittany on a leash on the far side of the gate or in a crate with a chew. Reward every glance at the cat that ends with your dog looking back at you. End the session while things are still easy.

Stage 4: same room, dog on leash

Pick a room where the cat can reach a high platform or cat tree. Keep your dog on a secure leash and reward calm behavior; a second person helps, one for each animal. Keep sessions short and frequent, several times a day, and take your dog out of the room if it becomes highly excitable. Let the cat decide whether to come closer. Never hold, cage or carry the cat toward the dog; the SF SPCA warns that forcing proximity is stressful and does not help.

Stage 5: leash dragging, close supervision

RSPCA Australia suggests off-leash contact only after a few weeks of leashed meetings, and even then under close supervision. A trailing leash gives you a way to step in calmly without grabbing your dog. If your Brittany starts an intense chase, interrupt it and redirect to another activity, as the SF SPCA advises, and go back to Stage 4 for the next few sessions.

Stage 6: free time while you are home

Once leash-free sessions stay calm, let the animals share space while you are around. Keep the cat's safe room and high routes permanently. RSPCA Australia is clear that the two should not be left alone together until you are certain they tolerate each other, your dog won't chase the cat, and everyone is safe.

How do you stop a Brittany from chasing the cat at home?

Prevent the chase with management, and reward the opposite behavior: looking at the cat and then turning back to you, or lying on a mat while the cat walks past. Scolding after a chase does not teach the alternative and can make your dog more excited around the cat.

Interrupt the stare, not the sprint

The easiest moment to help your Brittany is the second before the chase, when the body freezes and the eyes lock on. Say your dog's name or your walk-away cue in a normal voice, reward the turn, and add distance if needed. If your dog cannot look away at all, you are too close; that is information, not failure.

Give the energy somewhere else to go

A bird dog with nothing to do will find the most interesting moving thing in the house. Short sniffing games, a stuffed chew on the mat, or a retrieve in the yard before cat time all help. The SF SPCA notes that some dogs with a strong predatory drive can, with diligence, learn to direct that drive to other outlets and stick to trained routines around the cat, but that this is tricky and does not work in every case.

Watch the cat as closely as the dog

RSPCA Australia expects some hissing and tail swishing early on that should settle after a few days, and warns against punishing either animal. Worry more about a cat that is too afraid to eat, drink or use the litter tray. In the survey Coren describes, cats were 7 times more likely to keep their distance from the dog or leave the area, and dogs were 4 times more likely than cats to try to start a friendly interaction. Expect your cat to be the slower one, and plan around that.

Young Brittany and kitten together

A puppy and a kitten can grow up as friends, but both are fragile and fast. Puppy mouthing that is harmless with people can frighten or hurt a small cat, so handle that separately; our Brittany puppy biting guide covers how to redirect it. Keep puppy-and-kitten time short, supervised and on a leash or behind a gate until both are older and calmer.

Brittany and cats checklist

  • The cat has a dog-free room with food, water, litter, a bed and a hiding place.
  • High perches or a cat tree exist in every room where the two will meet.
  • Your Brittany cannot reach the litter tray or the cat's food.
  • Scent and room swaps happen before the first face-to-face meeting.
  • Early meetings are on a leash, with a gate, crate or second person.
  • Sessions are short, happen after exercise, and end while both are calm.
  • You reward your dog for looking away from the cat, not only for sitting.
  • You move forward only after several calm sessions, and step back after a lunge or chase.
  • The cat is eating, drinking and using the tray normally before each step forward.
  • The two are never alone together until you are certain your dog won't chase.

Common mistakes when introducing a Brittany to a cat

Most setbacks come from rushing, from forcing contact, or from letting a chase happen and then hoping it fades. Each one is easy to avoid once you know it.

  • Meeting on day one: skipping the settling-in time and scent swaps puts both animals under pressure at once.
  • Holding the cat up to the dog: restraining a cat near a dog is stressful and, according to the SF SPCA, does not help.
  • Dropping the leash too early: off-leash contact belongs after weeks of calm leashed meetings, not after one good evening.
  • Training while your dog is wired: a Brittany fresh from the crate or bursting to go out is the hardest version of your dog to work with.
  • Punishing hissing or barking: it builds bad feelings about the other animal, which is the opposite of the goal.
  • Judging only the dog: a calm dog and a hiding cat is not progress yet.
  • Leaving them alone "just for a minute": a single chase in an empty house can undo weeks of careful work.

If your Brittany keeps lunging despite slow steps, or your cat stays too frightened to eat or use the tray, RSPCA Australia recommends getting help from a trained behaviorist. Asking early is a sign of good care, not of failure.

FAQ: Brittany and cats

Are Brittanys good with cats?

Many can be, but it depends on the individual dog and cat more than on the breed. A Brittany who has lived calmly with cats before, or who stays curious and polite on a leash during a trial meeting, is a good prospect. Plan for a slow, managed introduction either way.

How long does it take to introduce a Brittany to a cat?

RSPCA Australia expects introductions to take at least a week and perhaps a few weeks, with off-leash contact only after a few weeks of leashed meetings. Let the more worried animal set the pace rather than the calendar.

Is it better to get a kitten or an adult cat with a Brittany?

Temperament matters most. The SF SPCA rates relaxed cats and kittens as the best prospects because they are less likely to flee, while RSPCA Australia notes that older cats are usually calmer than kittens. A confident cat that doesn't bolt is the easier match.

My Brittany stares and points at the cat. Is that dangerous?

A fixed stare shows strong interest and is the moment just before a chase, so treat it as a signal to add distance and reward your dog for looking back at you. It is not proof your dog wants to harm the cat, but it does mean you are not ready for the next stage.

Can my Brittany and cat ever be left alone together?

Only when you are certain they tolerate each other and your dog won't chase, as RSPCA Australia advises. Until then, supervise directly or keep them in separate rooms, and always leave the cat an area the dog cannot reach.

What if my Brittany and cat never become friends?

That can still be a success. RSPCA Australia notes that some pets never become friends but can live together by spending most of their time apart, with separate rooms, separate feeding and a safe sleeping spot for the cat.

A calm, managed home for your Brittany and cat

Living with a Brittany and cats is less about the breed and more about the plan you follow. Give the cat its own safe space and high routes, keep early meetings short and leashed, reward your dog for turning away from the cat, and only move forward when both animals are relaxed. Progress may feel slow, but every calm session is a step toward a home where your bird dog and your cat can simply share the evening.
