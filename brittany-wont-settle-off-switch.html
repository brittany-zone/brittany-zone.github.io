Brittany Won't Settle in the House: How to Teach an Off Switch to a Dog That Is Still Wired After a Long Walk

Use the minutes after a walk to teach calm on a mat, not more miles.

Home›Blog›Brittany won't settle

When a Brittany won't settle in the house after a long walk, the problem is rarely too little exercise; it is a missing off switch. You come home, unclip the leash, and your dog keeps pacing, following you from room to room, or bringing toys as if the walk never happened. This guide shows how to use that post-walk window to teach a calm settle on a mat, instead of letting it turn into another nightly loop of restlessness.

Quick answer: why won't my Brittany settle after a long walk?

Because settling is a skill, not an automatic result of being tired. A Brittany can come home physically worn and still be mentally switched on, so the fix is a short, rewarded routine on a dedicated mat right after the walk, practiced daily until lying down and relaxing becomes the expected next step.

TL;DR: Give the walk a clear ending, wait for breathing to slow, then run a 2–5 minute mat session that rewards relaxed body language, not just a down. Lengthen the wait in small steps, release your dog before they get up on their own, and repeat at the same time every day. The window right after you get home is the one most owners miss, and it is exactly when a settle is easiest to teach.

Why is a Brittany still wired after a long walk?

A Brittany is a working bird dog, so a walk full of scent, movement and birds can leave the brain busier than the legs are tired. The breed's history explains the intensity; it does not mean your dog cannot learn to relax indoors.

A dog built to keep working

The breed comes from the Brittany region of north-western France and is described as a gun dog of spaniel type bred primarily for bird hunting, one that historically both pointed and retrieved. A dog selected for long days of searching does not come with a built-in "walk over, nap now" setting. If your route goes past hedges full of sparrows or ducks on a pond, your Brittany may spend the whole outing scanning, and arrive home with that scanning still running.

If birds are the trigger on your walks, the transition starts before you even reach the door. Our guide to teaching a walk-away cue when your Brittany points on leash covers that part, so the walk ends with your dog moving with you rather than staring back at the last bird.

Why more miles rarely fix it

The common reaction is to walk farther. That can help a dog who is genuinely under-exercised, and our article on how much exercise a Brittany needs each day helps you check that first. But adding distance to a dog who already gets plenty can simply build a fitter dog who still paces at 9 p.m. Exercise and relaxation are separate skills, and only one of them is practiced on a walk.

Arousal does not switch off on its own schedule

Think of the post-walk period as a transition, not an end point. Fast play, a busy street, or a run-in with another dog can leave your Brittany alert long after the leash is off. Without a familiar routine, many dogs fill that gap with whatever got attention before: nudging, toy delivery, barking at you, or patrolling windows. Each evening that pattern repeats, it becomes the routine your dog expects.

What does a settled Brittany actually look like?

A settled dog is not just lying down; the body softens. Look for weight shifted onto one hip, the head lowered or resting, and slower, deeper breathing.

A down with the head up, eyes tracking you and hind legs tucked ready to spring is a pause, not a settle. Rewarding that posture alone teaches a quick down, which is useful, but it is not the off switch you want. The relaxation exercises handout from Today's Veterinary Practice lists the signs worth rewarding: hips shifted to one side, a leg stretched out, a shoulder lowered to the floor, lying on one side, the head lowered or the chin resting between the paws, plus sighing, closing the eyes, or slower breathing you can see in the ribcage.

How do you teach the off switch step by step?

Teach a mat cue in short sessions, reward relaxed body language on the mat, then slowly add time, distance and distractions. Once the mat routine works in a quiet room, move it to the minutes right after a walk.

Phase 1: make the mat worth visiting

Choose one mat, blanket or towel that you only bring out for this exercise. Battersea's settle guide starts by rewarding a dog for simply investigating the mat, tossing treats onto it rather than handing them over, then building from two paws on the mat to four, then a down. Battersea also recommends practicing when your dog is already tired, which is why the post-walk window works so well once the basics are in place.

Phase 2: shape the down into relaxation

Today's Veterinary Practice suggests training sessions of 2 to 5 minutes, 2 to 3 times a day, and notes that even one 30-second session a day can still make progress. Wait until your dog sits or lies down on the mat automatically about 8 out of 10 times before you add a word such as "mat" or "settle". Then start rewarding the softer postures from the table: a hip roll, a lowered head, a sigh.

Phase 3: add time in tiny steps

The same handout advises raising the wait before each reward by 5 to 10 seconds at a time, and stepping back at least 5 seconds if your dog breaks position. Battersea's version is to delay the reward by a couple of seconds at first and to go at your dog's pace. Deliver rewards slowly and calmly between the front paws; a fast, excited treat delivery can start the game all over again.

Phase 4: add distance, then the post-walk moment

Once your dog can stay relaxed on the mat for about 3 to 5 minutes, the handout suggests starting to add distance, one step at a time. At that point, move the session to the real problem: lay the mat down within a few minutes of coming home, and run the same short session you have already practiced in calm moments.

Release before your dog quits

End each session with a clear release word while your dog is still relaxed. If they always decide when the session ends by standing up, you are teaching a settle that lasts exactly as long as their patience. Short, successful sessions beat long ones that end in a scramble.

Which off-switch method fits your Brittany?

Most dogs do well with mat training plus rewarded relaxation; a leash-under-foot settle helps when you are away from home or your dog keeps leaving the mat. You can combine methods, as long as each has its own clear cue.

The leash-under-foot settle

Trainer Chirag Patel describes this method in his "Off" switch post on Dog Star Daily: start when your dog is already fairly calm, stand on the leash leaving about 2/3 of a foot of slack, and reward relaxing with a treat or a pre-stuffed chew toy. Sometimes he releases the dog as soon as it settles, so calming down quickly pays off. The verbal cue "settle" comes later, said just before stepping on the leash.

Chews as a bridge

A pre-stuffed chew on the mat gives a mouthy Brittany something calm to do while the body slows down. If your dog is young and the post-walk energy shows up as grabbing hands and sleeves, our Brittany puppy biting guide covers that separately; keep the mat session about lying down and relaxing.

How long should the post-walk wind-down take?

Plan on a few minutes of transition followed by a short mat session; this article sets no deadline for the whole skill. What matters is doing the same sequence every day, so your dog learns what comes next after the leash comes off.

Here is a practical routine you can copy. The times are this guide's suggestion for structuring the evening, not research thresholds.

  1. Last five minutes outside: slow the pace, allow some calm sniffing, and avoid starting fetch or a game at the door.
  2. Doorway: unclip the leash in the same spot every day, hang it up, and use a quiet tone. No big greeting, no toy toss.
  3. Water and quiet: offer water and give your dog a few minutes in a calm room while breathing slows. Don't start a training session while your dog is panting hard.
  4. Mat out: place the mat in the same spot. Its appearance is part of the cue.
  5. Short session: 2–5 minutes of calm rewards for relaxing on the mat, sized to your training stage (the planner above can time it).
  6. Release, then leave the mat down: give your release word, then sit nearby with a book. Quietly reward any relaxation your dog offers afterward.

What to do on days it falls apart

Some evenings, after a run-in with a loose dog or a long car ride, your Brittany will not get past the first column of the table. Skip the training that day: keep the house quiet, offer a chew, and try again tomorrow. Pushing for a long settle on a bad day mostly teaches your dog that the mat means frustration.

When "can't settle" means "can't be alone"

A dog who settles fine while you sit nearby but becomes distressed when you leave the room is dealing with something different. Our article on how common Brittany separation anxiety is explains the difference and the routine factors behind it. If you see that pattern, ask a qualified, reward-based trainer or behavior professional for help rather than stretching the mat exercise.

Post-walk settle checklist

  • The walk ends calmly, without a last-minute game of fetch.
  • The leash comes off in the same spot, with a quiet routine.
  • Water is available and your dog has a few minutes to slow down.
  • The mat appears only for settling, never for play.
  • Rewards go onto the mat or between the front paws, delivered slowly.
  • You reward relaxed postures, not just any down.
  • Waits grow by a few seconds at a time, and you step back after a break.
  • You release your dog before they get up on their own.
  • You note the session length and the calmest posture reached, so you can compare days.

Common mistakes that keep a Brittany wired

The biggest mistakes are expecting a long walk to do the work, rewarding the wrong moment, and making the mat exciting. Each one quietly trains the opposite of an off switch.

  • Walking farther every week: if exercise is already adequate, more miles build stamina, not calm.
  • Starting play at the door: a fetch game right after the walk tells your dog the excitement continues indoors.
  • Rewarding fast and loud: excited praise and rapid treats keep a working dog in work mode.
  • Waiting until the dog gives up: sessions that end with the dog walking away teach a short settle.
  • Scolding restlessness: it adds tension to a dog who is already struggling to relax.
  • Using the mat as a punishment spot: sending your dog there in anger turns the cue into something to escape.
  • Skipping calm days: the settle is easiest to build when nothing is going on; save the post-walk test for later.

FAQ: Brittany won't settle in the house

Is it normal that my Brittany won't settle in the house after a walk?

Many active dogs need help switching from outdoor work mode to indoor calm, and a Brittany's bird-hunting background can make that transition harder. It is a trainable skill: a short, daily mat routine teaches your dog what is expected after the leash comes off.

Should I just walk my Brittany longer?

Only if your dog is genuinely under-exercised. If daily exercise is already adequate, extra distance tends to build a fitter dog without teaching relaxation. Check exercise needs first, then teach the settle as a separate skill.

How long should a settle training session be?

Today's Veterinary Practice suggests sessions of 2 to 5 minutes, 2 to 3 times a day, and notes that even one 30-second session a day can still produce progress. End each session while your dog is still relaxed.

When should I start adding distance from the mat?

The same guidance suggests adding distance once your dog can stay relaxed on the mat for about 3 to 5 minutes, one step at a time. If your dog gets up, go back to an easier step.

Can I use a crate instead of a mat?

A crate can give a calm resting place, but this guide covers a portable mat cue that also works on visits, in cafés and anywhere else you go. If you use both, keep them as separate cues so each keeps a clear meaning.

What if my Brittany keeps getting off the mat?

Make the step easier: shorter waits, more frequent calm rewards, and a quieter room. Battersea recommends going back a step whenever your dog leaves the mat or breaks the down, then building up again at your dog's pace.

Turn the post-walk window into your off switch

If your Brittany won't settle in the house, look at what happens in the first minutes after the walk rather than adding more miles. Give the walk a calm ending, practice a short mat session that rewards real relaxation, and release your dog before they quit. Done daily, that small window becomes the off switch your evenings have been missing.
